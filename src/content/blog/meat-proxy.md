If you've spent any time on tech Twitter or Hacker News in the last month, you've seen the term. **Meat proxy**: a person who takes an AI system's output — a Slack reply, a pull request summary, a paragraph of code — and forwards it to someone else without reading it, understanding it, or checking it first. The human is just meat. The model did the thinking. The person in between did nothing but relay.

Developer Niklas Gruhn coined it in an August 2026 essay, *"Don't be a meat proxy,"* after getting tired of receiving verbatim Claude output in code reviews and group chats. Simon Willison amplified it the same day. The post hit the Hacker News front page with over 1,800 points, and within a week "meat proxy" was showing up in Forbes buzzword roundups next to its sibling term, **workslop** — AI-generated content that looks finished but falls apart the moment anyone actually engages with it.

The pairing is the whole story: someone generates workslop, a meat proxy forwards it unread, and whoever's on the receiving end does the verification work that should've happened two steps earlier. Researchers surveying US desk workers found roughly 40% had received workslop in the past month, at a cost of nearly two hours per incident untangling it.

***

### The diagnosis everyone's making is slightly wrong

Most of the discourse frames this as a discipline problem — people are lazy, people don't read, people should feel bad about pasting unverified output into a PR. I don't think that's it, or at least it's not the useful part of it.

The actual claim buried in the meat proxy discourse is an economic one: **generation got cheap, verification did not.** A model can produce a thousand words or two hundred lines of code in seconds. Nothing about that changes how long it takes a human to actually check whether those words or that code are correct. The bottleneck didn't go away when generation got fast — it just moved, silently, onto whichever human is standing closest to the output. Calling that person a meat proxy blames them for standing in a spot the system design put them in.

This is the same failure mode I wrote about after building [PujaGuru's MCP booking agent](/work/pujaguru) and [AcademicShield's on-chain verification layer](/work/academicshield): a system tells you something happened, and nothing about the sentence proves it. A meat proxy forwarding an unread AI reply and an MCP tool call claiming "booked, you're confirmed" are the same bug wearing two different jobs. Neither one is a receipt. Both are just language, generated the same way as every other sentence a model produces, trusted for reasons that have nothing to do with whether it's true.

### Why "just read the output" doesn't fix it

The advice everyone's giving — read it, understand it, validate it, then write it in your own words — is correct and also not going to hold up, because it relies on the exact discipline the term itself is complaining humans don't have under deadline pressure. It's a prose-style fix for a systems problem. Rewriting an AI's output in your own words doesn't prove you checked it; it just makes the unread relay harder to spot.

I've spent enough time on database concurrency to recognize this pattern. "Please remember to read the row before you write it back" isn't a locking strategy — it's the thing you say before you build the locking strategy, because you already know people won't. Row-level locks and transactions don't ask for discipline. They make the unsafe path structurally unavailable.

Verification needs the same treatment. Not a norm, a checkpoint:

- **Required-evidence fields**, not prose. A PR description that has to link the test run, the diff summary, or the specific lines a human actually touched — not a paragraph that could have been generated by the same model that wrote the code.
- **Review gates that block on absence of evidence**, not on the presence of AI. The problem was never that a model wrote the first draft. It's that nothing downstream checks whether a human did anything after that.
- **Verbatim relay treated as legitimate for artifacts** — a stack trace, an exact error message, an agent-to-agent handoff — and treated as a violation everywhere a human is implicitly vouching for correctness. Those are different acts wearing the same paste command, and no policy that treats them identically will hold.

### The actual fix is structural, same as it always is

None of this is new advice, which is exactly the point. Idempotency keys, transactional boundaries, and audit trails are older than the framework most of us are writing in this year, and they're still the answer to a problem the industry keeps re-discovering and re-naming. "Meat proxy" is a new word for an old failure: a system optimized for throughput with no forcing function for correctness, staffed by a human who — correctly, rationally — behaves exactly the way the system incentivizes.

If your pipeline's only verification step is "someone will probably read this," you don't have a verification step. You have a human-shaped single point of failure, and calling them a meat proxy when it fails is easier than fixing the pipeline that put them there.
